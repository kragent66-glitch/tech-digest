# The October 13, 2026 Microsoft Lifecycle Cliff: Windows 10 ESU, Office LTSC 2021, and the New Price of Paying Monthly

Five separate Microsoft support clocks stop on October 13, 2026, and the same month the company began charging customers for paying their bills monthly. Together they define the most expensive week of the year for any organization still running a Microsoft estate on its old assumptions: the cost of standing still just went up, and the speed at which it compounds depends on decisions most teams have not yet made. This is not an AI story. It is a maintenance-and-renewal story, and the renewal math is where the money actually moves.

## The largest lifecycle date of 2026 lands on October 13

Microsoft's own end-of-support list puts an unusual amount on a single day. Per MetrixData 360's [October 2026 licensing roundup](https://metrixdata360.com/top-takes/microsoft-licensing-changes-october-2026), five lifecycle events land together on October 13, 2026:

- Windows 10 commercial Extended Security Updates (ESU) Year 1 coverage ends.
- Windows 10 2016 LTSB and Windows 10 IoT Enterprise LTSB 2016 reach end of support.
- The entire Office LTSC 2021 family goes out of support, including Project LTSC 2021 and Visio LTSC 2021, with no extended support afterwards.
- Windows Server 2012 and Windows Server 2012 R2 reach the end of Extended Security Update Year 3, the final year on offer.
- Windows Server 2022 reaches end of mainstream support.

The reason this date is easy to under-plan for is that it is not a single cliff. It is five different mechanisms with five different remedies, and treating them as one line item in a budget spreadsheet is how organizations end up surprised. Some of these are hard stops, some are paid extensions, and one is not a stop at all.

## Windows 10 ESU is not ending the way most people think

Two separate Windows 10 programs share names and dates, and they are moving in opposite directions.

For businesses, ESU is a paid, per-device, cumulative subscription. Microsoft lists commercial ESU at [$61 per device for Year 1](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-windows-10-esu-cheat-sheet), doubling each year for up to three years. Year 1 covers through October 13, 2026, Year 2 through October 12, 2027 at $122 per device, and Year 3 through October 10, 2028 at $244 per device. ESU is cumulative and cannot be back-dated around: a business that skipped Year 1 and wants Year 2 coverage pays both, a $183 per device catch-up, and the full three-year path costs $427 per device. Education customers pay a fraction of that, at $1, $2, and $4 for the three years respectively.

The commercial point is the doubling. Year 1 was cheap enough to treat as a cheap extension while a Windows 11 rollout stalled. Year 2 is where the arithmetic starts to compete with simply replacing or upgrading the hardware, and Year 3 is where ESU stops being a workaround and starts being a strategy you have to justify.

Consumer ESU, meanwhile, went the other way. In June 2026 Microsoft [quietly extended](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/06/26/microsoft-windows-10-free-security-updates-esu-program) the consumer Extended Security Updates program to October 12, 2027, a year past its original October 13, 2026 end date. The change appeared as an edit to Microsoft's ESU documentation and an editor's note on a Windows Experience blog post rather than a formal announcement. Enrollment options did not change: sync PC settings through Windows Backup at no cost, redeem 1,000 Microsoft Rewards points, or pay $30 for a one-year license that covers up to 10 devices on one account. So the two programs that carry the "October 13, 2026" date are doing opposite things: consumer coverage just got a free extension, and commercial coverage just moved into its most expensive phase.

## Office LTSC 2021: the cliff with no safety net

Office LTSC 2021 is the cleanest hard stop on the list, and the one with the least room to maneuver. Per [Microsoft's lifecycle announcement](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/announcements/office-ltsc-2021-end-of-support), support for Office LTSC 2021 ends October 13, 2026. After that date Microsoft provides no technical support, no bug fixes, and no security updates. There is no Extended Security Updates program for it, and no extended support phase. The software keeps running, but every vulnerability discovered after the date stays unpatched permanently.

That distinction matters because it is the opposite of the Windows Server 2022 and Windows 10 ESU situations. Office LTSC 2021 goes straight from supported to unsupported, with no paid bridge. Microsoft's Message Center post MC1472429, published September 15, 2026, confirmed the same date for Office LTSC 2021, Visio LTSC 2021, and Project LTSC 2021. The recommended paths are Microsoft 365 Apps for cloud-first organizations, or Office LTSC 2024 for those that need a perpetual, on-premises version. The window is the risk: any device still on LTSC 2021 after October 13 is running unpatched productivity software that touches documents, macros, and email.

## Windows Server: two very different October 13 outcomes

The two Windows Server events on October 13 look alike on a date list and behave nothing alike in practice.

Windows Server 2012 and 2012 R2 hit the end of ESU Year 3, which is the final year Microsoft offers. For these platforms, October 13, 2026 is a genuine end of patching: no Year 4 exists. Organizations still running them need to have migrated or accepted permanent exposure.

Windows Server 2022 is the opposite. October 13, 2026 is the end of mainstream support only, and security updates continue under extended support [through October 14, 2031](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/5785785/windows-server-eol). What actually changes is subtler and easier to miss: free non-security hotfixes, complimentary support incidents, and warranty claims for non-security issues stop. As [Bellator Cyber notes](https://bellatorcyber.com/blog/windows-server-2022-end-of-mainstream-support), a misbehaving driver, a performance bug, or a compatibility quirk unrelated to a vulnerability may no longer get a free fix. The risk is organizational rather than immediate: unpatched instances, forgotten inventory, and compliance documentation gaps that surface during an audit or after an incident. The migration target is Windows Server 2025, and the correct move is to plan it on your own timeline rather than a forced one.

## The CSP 5% billing-cadence uplift: when paying monthly became a priced product

The second half of October 2026 is a pricing change, not a lifecycle one, and it is the more consequential of the two because it is quiet by design.

From October 1, 2026, Microsoft applies a [5% cost-of-capital uplift](https://cspcontrolcenter.com/blog/microsoft-csp-software-price-uplift-october-2026) to annual-term Microsoft Cloud Solution Provider (CSP) software subscriptions that are billed monthly. Microsoft names SQL Server, Windows Server, Client Access Licenses (CALs), and System Center as examples, but has published no closed product list, so the real trigger is the billing shape: annual term, billed monthly. Annual billing on the same annual term is unaffected, and month-to-month terms are unaffected. The uplift applies at each subscription's renewal on or after October 1, 2026, never mid-term.

What makes this hard to manage is that it is not a list-price increase. A list-price increase has one effective date and a defined product set. This change lands at each subscription's individual renewal, so a single announcement behaves like twelve months of smaller surprises, each too small on its own to trigger a review. Microsoft requires no Partner Center configuration change, which is exactly what pushes the entire consequence downstream into price lists, quotes, renewal workflows, and invoices, where most organizations have nobody assigned to catch it. A quote issued in September for an October renewal is understated by 5%, and a typical quoting workflow will not flag it. The error surfaces at invoice, in front of the customer.

There is precedent. Microsoft announced in November 2024 that from April 1, 2025 it would apply a 5% premium to annual-term seat-based cloud subscriptions billed monthly, covering Microsoft 365, Office 365, Enterprise Mobility and Security, Windows 365, Dynamics 365, and Power Platform. October 2026 extends the same mechanism to on-premises software. Read together, the two changes describe a consistent direction of travel: payment cadence is becoming a priced commercial term across the Microsoft catalog, not a concession partners can keep giving away without pricing it.

## Why a 5% cost increase is not a 5% problem

A 5% increase in cost is applied to the largest number in the transaction and absorbed by the smallest one, which means the share of profit it removes depends entirely on how thin the margin was to begin with. The arithmetic below is derived from the stated 5% uplift, not a Microsoft estimate:

| Your resale margin | Share of gross profit the uplift removes | Sell-price rise to hold margin percentage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5% | 95% | 5.0% |
| 8% | 58% | 5.0% |
| 10% | 45% | 5.0% |
| 15% | 28% | 5.0% |

On a 15% resale margin, a 5% cost increase removes roughly 28% of gross profit. On a 10% margin it removes 45%. To hold margin percentage, a partner raises the sell price by the full 5%. To hold gross profit in currency instead, the required rise is smaller, roughly 3.75% to 4.75% depending on the starting margin, because the goal is a fixed dollar floor rather than a fixed percentage. Either rule is defensible. What is not defensible is letting the uplift come out of gross profit by default, one renewal at a time, with no approval step and no alert. A customer can also avoid the uplift entirely by moving to annual billing on the same annual term, since the charge prices the cost of monthly collection rather than the license itself. That makes it a commercial option to offer, not a default to impose.

## Why this is a maintenance-and-renewal story, not an AI story

October 2026's Microsoft news has been dominated by AI, from gated frontier models to consumption-based Copilot pricing. The lifecycle and pricing changes above will move more enterprise money than any single model release this quarter, precisely because they hit the boring lines in the budget: the old office suite, the old server, the old workstation OS, and the billing cadence nobody agreed to renegotiate.

The pattern is consistent and worth internalizing. Microsoft is converting fixed, one-off relationships into recurring, priced ones, whether that is a $61 device going to $122 going to $244, or monthly billing becoming a paid attribute rather than a courtesy. The organizations that handle October 13 well will not be the ones with the biggest AI budgets. They will be the ones that can answer, against their own inventory rather than a public date list, which devices still run Office LTSC 2021, which servers sit on the final year of ESU, and which Microsoft lines were on a promotion that expired September 30, 2026, now that the renewal quote has changed. The dates are public and easy. The exposure is private and specific, and that is the part that costs money.

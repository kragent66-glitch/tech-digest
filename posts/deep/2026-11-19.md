# Anthropic IPO Prices at $83 a Share: Inside the $81 Billion Listing and the $895 Billion Compute Bet

Anthropic priced its initial public offering at $83 per Class A share on November 18, 2026, above the $70 to $78 range it had marketed, and upsized the deal to 980 million shares. The offering raised about $81.3 billion and values the Claude maker at roughly $1.5 trillion on a fully diluted basis, according to [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/anthropic-clinches-biggest-ever-ipo-with-81-billion-offering-2026-11-18). Shares begin trading on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker ANT on Thursday, November 19. The listing matters beyond its size: it is the first time public markets can price a frontier AI lab directly, and the prospectus behind it discloses a business growing faster than almost any software company in history while carrying close to $895 billion of committed compute spend. Buyers are not just underwriting Claude. They are underwriting a fixed-cost infrastructure bet that has to be fed for a decade.

## What Anthropic actually sold

The final terms differ from the ones filed on November 10. That amended prospectus set 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78, which would have raised $57.75 billion to $64.35 billion and implied a fully diluted valuation of about $1.404 trillion at the top, as [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/10/anthropic-sets-ipo-price-range-of-70-to-78-per-share-to-reach-an-implied-valuation-of-1-404-trillion). Demand then ran ahead of the range. On November 18, [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/18/anthropic-ipo-ai-offering-price.html) reported that Anthropic was weighing an upsized deal and a price well above $80, with demand stronger than what SpaceX saw in June.

The final structure, per [Yahoo Finance's account of the pricing](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/anthropic-prices-ipo-83-share-014000480.html):

| Term | Final figure |
| --- | --- |
| Price per Class A share | $83.00 |
| Shares offered | 980 million (about 956.4 million new, 23.6 million from existing holders) |
| Gross IPO proceeds | About $81.3 billion |
| Underwriters' option | Up to 147 million additional shares, about $12.2 billion |
| Fully diluted valuation | About $1.5 trillion |
| Concurrent private placement | $26 billion of non-voting Class N stock at the IPO price |
| Lead underwriters | Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, in a 22-bank syndicate |
| Ticker and exchange | ANT, Nasdaq Global Select Market |

The private placement is the part worth reading twice. Google and Nvidia each committed about $10 billion, AMD about $2.5 billion, Broadcom, Micron and Microsoft about $1 billion each, and TSMC about $500 million, all at the IPO price and all in non-voting stock, according to the [amended S-1 as summarized by FinanceFeeds](https://financefeeds.com/anthropic-ipo-date-price-range-70-78-trading-19-november). The structure gives Anthropic capital and its chip and cloud partners a locked-in stake in its survival, which is comfortable for Anthropic and awkward for anyone worried about circular financing.

Adding the placement to the IPO takes gross new capital above $107 billion. Reuters described the $81.31 billion IPO as the largest stock-market debut on record. SpaceX's June listing is reported at roughly $75 billion before its option and up to about $86 billion once the option is counted, so the record depends on which figure is used.

## The revenue curve is real, the loss is mostly accounting

The financials in the amended filing explain why the book built. Revenue for the nine months ended September 30, 2026 was $33.24 billion, up about 1,200 percent from $2.55 billion a year earlier, according to [CNBC's read of the filing](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/02/anthropic-ant-s-1-quarter-financials.html). Third-quarter revenue was roughly $17 billion, up about 1,127 percent year over year. The company also disclosed run-rate revenue of about $81 billion as of October 31.

The growth is decelerating in sequence even as the absolute numbers climb. CNBC calculated quarter-over-quarter growth of about 132 percent in Q1, 144 percent in Q2, and 47 percent in Q3. That is normal at this scale, and it is the number that matters most for a valuation near $1.5 trillion: at $81 billion of run-rate revenue, buyers are paying roughly 18.5 times annualized sales for a business whose sequential growth just fell by two thirds.

The headline loss looks catastrophic and mostly is not. Anthropic reported a $104 billion net loss for the nine months, against $25.1 billion a year earlier, and most of it came from remeasuring convertible notes and derivative liabilities at fair value, a non-cash charge of about $94.3 billion. Strip that out and the picture is close to breakeven: an operating loss of about $1.26 billion for nine months, interest income of about $2.36 billion, and a Q2 with the company's first quarterly operating profit, per [Luminix's analysis](https://www.useluminix.com/reports/company-overviews/anthropic-revenue-and-burn-rate). Anthropic holds roughly $132 billion in cash and short-term investments and carries $6.2 billion of debt, which the [Associated Press noted](https://spectrumlocalnews.com/nys/capital-region/business/2026/11/18/anthropic-ipo-price) it describes in filings as substantial.

A $104 billion GAAP loss on $33 billion of revenue is still a disclosure problem, and Anthropic has to explain the gap between a mark-to-model charge and cash burn for as long as those notes stay on the balance sheet.

## The $895 billion commitment stack

The obligation side of the balance sheet is where the IPO story gets interesting. Anthropic disclosed purchase commitments and lease agreements totaling about $895.4 billion, "substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure," and said about $70 billion of that comes due in the next 12 months. Compute and infrastructure expenses rose by $14.4 billion in the nine months, up 329 percent year over year.

The [MLQ breakdown of the commitments](https://mlq.ai/news/anthropics-s-1-shows-a-fast-growing-ai-company-built-on-extraordinary-compute-obligations) shows how distributed the dependency is:

| Counterparty | Committed spend | Term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Google | At least $114.5 billion | April 2026 to July 2033 |
| Amazon | At least $110 billion | May 2026 to April 2036 |
| SpaceXAI | Up to $120.5 billion | Through 2029 |
| Broadcom | About $161.2 billion | Multi-year |
| Microsoft | At least $44.9 billion | November 2026 to May 2033 |
| Nscale | Up to $44.6 billion | Multi-year |
| Lambda | About $41.3 billion | Multi-year |

The SpaceXAI number works out to roughly $3.1 billion per month once all capacity is delivered, which Anthropic expects in December 2026. That is a rent bill payable whether or not customers show up. The filing also flags that about 80 percent of first-half revenue was consumption-based, meaning it can fall if customers cut usage or route work to cheaper models, while many of the compute commitments are fixed.

This is the central tension of the equity story. Anthropic is not selling a software margin. It is selling a spread between contracted capacity and resold tokens, and that spread depends on utilization, model efficiency, and inference prices holding up. Efficiency gains cut the cost of serving but also cut what customers expect to pay, so volume has to outrun both.

## Who pays, and how concentrated the book is

Anthropic's customer metrics are the strongest part of the filing. Customers spending $100,000 or more on a trailing twelve-month basis grew from more than 950 a year earlier to more than 11,900 as of September 30, 2026. That cohort produced $24.5 billion of revenue in the first nine months, about 74 percent of the total, according to the S-1/A. The company also disclosed more than 1,900 customers above $1 million and roughly 250 above $10 million, with net revenue retention near 900 percent.

That retention number deserves skepticism as much as admiration. Nine times more spend from the same accounts is plausible for enterprises scaling pilots into production, and impossible to sustain. It is a snapshot of a land grab, not a steady state.

Concentration runs through the distribution channel too. Much of Anthropic's revenue reaches customers through Amazon and Google marketplaces, and those two are also equity holders, compute suppliers, and competitors with their own frontier models. The IPO does not resolve that entanglement. It prices it.

## Governance: a controlled company by design

Buyers of Class A stock get economic exposure and very little control. Anthropic is a Delaware public benefit corporation. A single share of Class F stock held by an entity owned by the seven co-founders carries 50.1 percent of voting power on most matters, and a Long-Term Benefit Trust holds a Class T share that elects a majority of the board. Class N shares from the private placement carry no general voting rights. The company expects to qualify as a controlled company under Nasdaq rules.

Most existing holders are locked up for up to 180 days, with co-founders and larger holders locked up for up to 365 days, subject to early-release provisions. The float will be thin at first, which supports the price on the way up and amplifies any rush for the exit in May 2027.

The filing also carries more than 90 pages of risk factors, including political headwinds, infrastructure dependency, and the tension between safety commitments and commercial pressure. The co-founders call it a personal responsibility to help the world understand model risks, but Class A holders have no mechanism to enforce that.

## Why the deal priced above range in a nervous market

The macro backdrop made the pricing a genuine test. Ten days earlier, on November 12, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO, and board member effective immediately, with CFO Colette Kress named interim CEO and director Harvey C. Jones appointed the board's first chair, as [Constellation Research documented](https://www.constellationr.com/insights/news/nvidia-ceo-huang-resigns-kress-becomes-interim-ceo-looming-questions-abound). The company gave no reason, and reporting around the exit concerned personal conduct and an internal investigation.

Nvidia then reported, on November 17, revenue of $115.8 billion for its fiscal third quarter, up 103 percent year over year, with data center revenue of $107.6 billion, up 110 percent, and guided the fourth quarter to $130 billion while assuming no data center compute revenue from China. Vera Rubin NVL72, the company said, is the fastest product ramp in its history, according to the [earnings release](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-Financial-Results-for-Third-Quarter-Fiscal-2027).

So the demand signal and the governance shock arrived in the same week: a supplier growing at triple digits under an interim CEO, and a customer pricing the largest IPO ever into that uncertainty. Anthropic's book answered the question. The deal priced 6.4 percent above the top of its range and grew by 155 million shares. For a company with a $104 billion GAAP loss and $895 billion of commitments, that is a remarkable vote of confidence in the revenue line.

The comparison to OpenAI is the other lens. OpenAI was reportedly valued at $1.25 trillion in a November round after raising $11 billion, roughly half of what it first sought, and is not expected to list until 2027. Anthropic is now public at a higher mark with a smaller private raise behind it.

## What to watch after the first trade

Four things will determine whether $83 looks cheap or expensive a year from now.

The first is the first-day print. IPO prices are set by bankers allocating shares, not by the market clearing volume. A pop is a subsidy to the buyers who got allocation, not a verdict on the company. What matters is where ANT trades after the initial flow, and whether it holds above the offer.

The second is the November quarter. Anthropic's sequential growth fell from 144 percent to 47 percent. If Q4 steps down toward 30 percent while commitments keep accruing at roughly $3 billion a month, the 18.5 times run-rate multiple will look exposed.

The third is delivery of capacity. The $895.4 billion is a commitment, not a capability. Anthropic depends on Amazon, Google, SpaceXAI, Microsoft and a set of neoclouds to turn capital into working accelerators on schedule. Any slip in a large lease pushes revenue out while the payment stays.

The fourth is the lockup calendar and the politics. Roughly 180 days from pricing lands in mid-May 2027, and a Democratic-led Congress has signaled scrutiny of data center buildout and AI concentration. A public benefit corporation with a trust-controlled board is an unusual thing to defend in a hearing.

None of this makes the listing a mistake. Anthropic built a $33 billion nine-month revenue base in five years and raised equity instead of adding much more debt. But the IPO does not validate the business model so much as publish it. The $895 billion of commitments is now a number every public investor can read, and every quarter from here will be judged against it.

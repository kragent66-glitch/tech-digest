# The Verification Bottleneck: AI Proofs, the Riemann Hypothesis, and the Check That Matters

On November 20, 2026, an Anthropic mathematician named Levent Alpöge published a claim that he and the company's Claude models had discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, plus a method that resolves its generalized form. Roughly two days later, the Lean and Mathlib communities said they had "full confidence" that the proof is correct and certified both its statement and its proof. That certification is the headline. The more consequential story is what it reveals: AI systems can now generate mathematical results faster than the human field can verify them, and mathematics is the first discipline where that gap is visible, measurable, and being argued over in public. The bottleneck in science has moved from discovery to checking.

## The claim that moved faster than the review

The Riemann hypothesis dates to 1859. It states that the non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function all lie on a single vertical line, a pattern that would explain the irregular distribution of the primes. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, each backed by $1 million from the Clay Mathematics Institute.

Alpöge, who is also a junior fellow at Harvard's Society of Fellows, framed the result carefully. "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," he wrote in a note accompanying the release. He credits the division of labor honestly. He steered the conceptual strategy, moving a technique from a toy function-field setting toward its integer analogue. Claude supplied expertise he says he lacks, particularly on automorphic forms for higher-rank groups. The method builds on Stepanov's function-field approach and repeated theta lifts in an automorphic framework.

The evidence trail is unusually concrete. He released the argument as a Lean 4 formalization, reported at 17 million lines and split into 14 compressed archive parts. He chose not to publish a conventional prose write-up, describing a generated version that ran past 250,000 pages as unreadable. The timeline, which he cautions was partly paraphrased by Claude and may need revision, runs from a Stepanov determinant on October 6 to the final generalized result on November 16.

This was not the first signal. On August 10, 2026, Anthropic reported that an unreleased research version of Claude had raised a longstanding lower bound for the proportion of zeta zeros satisfying the hypothesis from 41.6% to 67.2%. That work explicitly did not prove the hypothesis. It used two Claude Code sessions and 31 million output tokens; Claude generated 650 ideas that failed before coordinating about 60 subagents on a second attempt. The November claim presents a complete proof through a different route.

## Why Lean is both the breakthrough and the trap

Lean is a programming language and proof assistant in which a small trusted kernel checks every logical step. A proof is code. If the file compiles with no `sorry` placeholder, the theorem as stated is proved. Most of the underlying mathematics lives in Mathlib, the community library, which on October 16, 2026 reported 290,462 theorems, 138,028 definitions and 772 contributors.

The community did not simply take Alpöge's word for it. It checked the work against four criteria that Kevin Buzzard's Xena Project blog set out on November 12 for judging AI-generated Lean code: whether the Lean statement matches the real theorem, whether the code compiles, whether it proves that statement, and whether the proof itself is valid. The Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative endorsed a joint statement saying all four are met.

The details matter. The statement of the hypothesis is not Alpöge's own. It is the definition already in Mathlib, vetted by number theorists. The community added a file stating the hypothesis importing only Mathlib, then compared it with the theorem in Alpöge's library using two independent tools, comparator and con-leche's compare mode. Both found an exact match. Dozens of people built the codebase and reproduced the same raw proof export, which was then checked by Lean's official kernel, by the con-leche and con-ron checkers, and by several third-party checkers written in different languages. MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland reports that the five headline theorems depend only on Lean's three standard axioms.

That statement check is not a formality. A Lean proof is only as good as its headline statement. Buzzard's post gives the example of a two-line Lean version of Fermat's Last Theorem that compiles but is trivially false, because Lean's natural numbers include zero. A proof of the wrong statement can compile perfectly. Machine verification settles correctness. It does not settle understanding. The community statement closes by looking forward to work "that will help explain the new ideas in this proof," and two checks, a comparator run and one Lean-kernel replay, were still finishing as of 04:35 UTC on November 22.

The result also arrived early. Forecasters on Metaculus had put the expected date at May 2035, and Terence Tao said last month he expected the problem to fall to a combination of humans and AI around 2050. The Clay institute's rules still require publication in a qualifying outlet, two years of waiting, and general acceptance, and it does not accept direct submissions. No Clay statement on the proof was found.

## The flood: 722 proofs and a pile nobody asked for

The Riemann claim landed on top of a much larger wave. In October 2026, OpenAI released 722 mathematical manuscripts produced by an unreleased internal model, posted publicly on GitHub with reasoning traces and Lean verification files. The releases span 17 subject areas, from prime number theory to plasma physics to quantum circuits, and include partial progress related to the Riemann hypothesis and the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture. None of the Millennium Prize Problems were solved outright. The pace is the story: output went from roughly 10 results in August to over 100 in September to 722 by early October, roughly 10x growth per month, with each manuscript reportedly costing about three hours of compute.

OpenAI has since funded math workshops to vet 372 result families, working with an IAS-hosted advisory group. But the numbers expose the structural problem. Terence Tao described the October release as a pile the community did not ask for and now has to sort through. In a September 11, 2026 essay titled "A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics," he called it "disturbing" to see proofs "abandoned at an intermediate stage," singling out cases where a company uses AI to generate and verify a proof but then shows "no inclination" to develop it further, to give talks, or to go through peer review. He proposed making an old norm explicit: an author seeking credit for generating a proof should commit to making their best efforts to develop it.

The 2026 Leiden Declaration on AI in mathematics names the same threat first. Current automated techniques, it warns, can produce plausible but unreliable or even incorrect arguments that are difficult to distinguish from correct proofs, and this applies not only to informal arguments but also to formalizations.

## Who actually checks the machines

Generating a proof and confirming one are different problems. A model can produce a document that looks like rigorous mathematics, uses correct notation, and follows plausible steps, without being valid. Lean helps with internal validity once a proof is translated into its language. It cannot tell you whether a result is meaningful, novel, or a reformulation that misses the point.

That judgment requires a specialist. For many of the subfields covered in the October release, only a few hundred to roughly a thousand mathematicians worldwide may have the background to evaluate a single result properly, and a thorough review can take days to weeks per paper. Multiply that pool across 722 manuscripts, and the backlog becomes the constraint. Math is the favorable case, because its outputs can in principle be mechanically checked. Biology, physics, chemistry and economics have no Lean-equivalent, where confirming a claim often requires lab replication, instrumentation, time and money.

## From pure math to the wet lab

The same shift is arriving in experimental science, minus the safety net of formal proof. A paper in Nature Biotechnology in 2026 introduced RhoAgent, an agentic system that designs functional RNA molecules and iteratively refines them using experimental feedback. It couples a large language model, which forms hypotheses and interprets results, with RDiff, a diffusion-based generator conditioned on base interactions and functional motifs.

The reported results are concrete. RhoAgent generated active ribozymes with only 40% to 70% sequence similarity to their wild-type counterparts, enhanced the cleavage efficiency of the structurally uncharacterized hovlinc ribozyme by up to sixfold, improved fluorescence from a fluorogenic aptamer scaffold by 230% relative to wild type, and designed three-dimensional scaffolds positioning two distinct functional cores for aptamer-based FRET systems. The system is best described as a virtual scientist running a design-and-test loop.

Code verification is moving just as fast. Harmonic's Aristotle system resolved 96.8% of problems on VERINA, a code-verification benchmark built by UC Berkeley and Meta researchers, proving 160 of 189 formal specifications correct and finding 23 false. The US fusion lab DIII-D used it to rebuild part of its reactor-control software in four hours, starting from one research paper and a 254-word prompt. In January 2026, GPT-5.2 Pro paired with Aristotle produced a Lean proof described as the first Erdős problem fully resolved autonomously by an AI system.

## What verification-first science looks like

The lesson from the Riemann week is not that AI cannot do mathematics. It clearly can, and the community certification is a genuine technical milestone. The lesson is that verification is now the scarce resource, and the institutions around discovery have not caught up.

Three practices are emerging as the minimum bar. First, ship the formal artifact, not just the claim. Lean code can be checked by anyone with the toolchain, which is why the Riemann release could be certified in about 48 hours instead of years. Second, state check before proof check. The Mathlib comparison step, matching Alpöge's theorem to the vetted definition rather than a lookalike, is the part that guards against the elegant-but-wrong-statement failure mode. Third, accept the caretaker obligation. Tao's proposal is blunt: if you claim credit for a generated proof, commit to explaining it, presenting it, and putting it through review.

The deeper risk is a growing stack of claimed results that nobody has fully checked. That outcome does not depend on whether the individual proofs are correct. It depends on whether the field can keep a human community capable of following the reasoning. Mathematics got a working verification layer before it needed one. Every other science will need to build its own, or accept that its literature will increasingly contain claims that are formally tidy and substantively unexamined. The check, not the discovery, is where the next decade of scientific integrity will be won.

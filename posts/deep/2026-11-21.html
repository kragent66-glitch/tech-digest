The 2026 Patch Wave: How AI-Found Vulnerabilities Broke FAA Air Traffic, Microsoft 365 and NSA Net

Utkarsh Bhangale Nov 21, 2026 11 min read2,112 words

Key facts

In the space of eight days in November 2026, Microsoft 365 went dark for most of a working day, the FAA stopped every departing flight in the United States for roughly five hours, and the National Security Agency's classified internal network stayed degraded for three days.

In the space of eight days in November 2026, Microsoft 365 went dark for most of a working day, the FAA stopped every departing flight in the United States for roughly five hours, and the National Security Agency's classified internal network stayed degraded for three days. None of it was a cyberattack, according to the agencies involved. All of it was maintenance. That is the part worth understanding: the outages were the visible cost of a much larger, quieter event, a forced re-patching of the world's software at a tempo set by AI vulnerability discovery rather than by human release cycles. The White House fired its National Cyber Director over the sequence on November 19. The engineering problem that produced it is still there.

Seven days in November: the outage timeline

The incidents arrived close enough together that officials had to keep insisting they were unrelated. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said there was "zero evidence" the outages were connected and that "we are not under systemic attack," as the BBC reported. President Trump put it more plainly on November 20: "They're all separate events. We have thousands of companies that are upgrading right now. And when they upgrade, bad things happen," according to Global News.

Two of those rows are maintenance events that went wrong. The third, at the NSA, was described by the agency itself as maintenance. That pattern is the story.

The FAA ground stop: 22 centers, one overloaded router, an AI tool switched on three days earlier

The FAA issued the ground stop at 3:54pm Eastern on Friday November 13, citing "extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety." Airspace reopened at 8:15pm. It was the third nationwide ground stop in US history, after September 11, 2001 and a January 2023 system outage, per Al Jazeera.

The numbers: 4,700 flights cancelled and 10,000 delayed on the Friday, with 700 already cancelled for Saturday, according to FlightRadar24. Cirium counted 827 aircraft diverted and about 11,000 flights still scheduled to depart after 4pm.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed switches and routers that "became overloaded with data and went down," and said the incident did not involve a total loss of communications or radar. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin described it as the result of patching new technology into an old framework until the older system was overwhelmed. Duffy called the underlying system "incredibly old" and asked Congress for $50 billion in emergency funding to modernize it.

The coincidence that consumed the aviation press: three days earlier, on November 10, the FAA had expanded SMART, its AI-supported traffic planning tool, to all 22 air route traffic control centers. SMART, short for Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories, was built by Air Space Intelligence under an $875 million, 12-year contract. Duffy rejected the link in unusually absolute terms: "This is completely disconnected... this is not even plugged in. So, there's no possible way SMART would have impacted," he said. The vendor posted its own denial while the ground stop was still active. Mullin, asked whether the tool contributed, said "you can't rule out anything at this point," per Latin Times.

Treat the denials as claims, not findings. No root cause report has been published, and the FAA has not explained why redundant paths did not absorb a router failure. "The whole aviation community is based on redundancy," aviation law professor Ron Bartsch told Al Jazeera. "So the fact that a redundant system hasn't kicked in immediately just raises more questions than what we've seen answers to at this stage."

Why routine maintenance is now the dangerous part

Microsoft's incident is the clearest teaching case because the company published enough status detail to reconstruct the failure shape, even without a final root cause. The main incident, MO1550821, opened at 18:26 UTC on November 12. In Microsoft's words, quoted by BleepingComputer and The Register, "the elevated load isn't driven by an increase in traffic, but by a number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable; straining the remaining infrastructure." A core process on affected servers was "becoming overwhelmed." Servers that recovered went unresponsive again once they took traffic.

That is a classic cascading failure with a specific and under-appreciated trigger: the health check itself. Health checks exist to pull broken servers out of rotation. When many fail at once, their load moves to the survivors. If the survivors are near capacity, they fail too, and the removal accelerates. Traffic never rose. Capacity simply left.

The standard defenses are unglamorous. Spare headroom in every failure domain. Health checks that distinguish "broken" from "busy," so an overloaded but functioning server keeps its traffic. Admission control that sheds load instead of dying. A cap on how much capacity the health check system can drain at once, so a bug in the checker cannot empty a fleet. Cells with independent capacity. And staged rollouts with automatic pause.

Microsoft's own mitigation list maps onto several of these: it paused deployments, redistributed traffic, added capacity, switched to an alternative health check method, and extended the email delivery timeout to 48 hours. At one point its status updates admitted, "Actions taken thus far have not provided the anticipated relief." That sentence is what a control plane that has lost the ability to reason about its own state sounds like.

The supply side: 86,533 CVEs and a patch wave nobody sized for

The reason so much infrastructure is being changed at once is upstream, in the vulnerability pipeline. NVD listed 86,533 CVE records published between January 1 and October 25, 2026, against 48,151 for all of 2025, according to Brandefense's analysis. That is roughly 290 records a day versus 132 last year. CISA put the 2026 count above 67,000 in October and projected 96,000 for the year, The Record reported.

Google's threat intelligence group traced the curve month by month: disclosures rose from 5,045 in January to 10,740 in August, while high-risk findings went from about 65 a month in mid-2025 to about 135 a month in mid-2026. Exploited-in-the-wild vulnerabilities hit 141 in eight months, more than the 127 recorded in all of 2025, per Google Cloud.

Anthropic's own disclosure ledger makes the AI contribution concrete: 6,157 vulnerabilities across 591 open source projects, with 516 patched, as of October 2, published on red.anthropic.com. The findings come from Claude Mythos Preview, the model behind Project Glasswing. OpenAI's equivalent, Patch the Planet, led a coalition through critical PHP flaws found with GPT-5.6 Sol and committed $1 billion in subsidized access for defenders, the company said.

The monthly patch load has moved accordingly. ThreatDown counted 998 CVEs addressed around September 2026's Patch Tuesday, against 96 in the same window a year earlier, with Chrome patches up 36-fold and the Linux kernel's three-month rolling average tripled, per Campus Technology.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre has already named the phase. "AI... is showing the ability to exploit this technical debt at scale and at pace across the technology ecosystem," CTO Ollie Whitehouse wrote in a blog preparing organizations for a "vulnerability patch wave". The NCSC expects a "forced correction" across open source, commercial, proprietary and SaaS software, and tells operators to enable hot patching, enable automatic updates, and otherwise build processes that tolerate frequent, large-scale change.

The remediation gap: the patch queue is now the attack surface

Volume is not the only problem. Throughput is. Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report found that 35 percent of known, actively exploited vulnerabilities were still unpatched after 28 days in 2025. Every one of the new AI-found bugs enters the same queue, behind the same change advisory process, the same test environments and the same maintenance windows.

That is the collision the November outages exposed. Patching fast is a security control. Changing fast is an availability risk. For two decades, enterprises resolved the tension by patching slowly, accepting exploit risk in exchange for stability. AI moved the exploit side of that trade without moving the change side. The result is emergency change: updates compressed into windows too short for the validation that legacy systems need. Mullin's description of the FAA failure, new technology patched into an old framework until it buckled, is exactly what that compression looks like when a 30-year-old telecom stack is asked to absorb modern data volumes on a schedule set by someone else.

There is a second-order effect that matters for anyone running production systems. AI is now good enough to find bugs in the tools you use to deploy fixes. Anthropic's ledger includes findings in libssh2, wolfSSL and open62541, the kind of libraries that sit inside orchestration and networking gear. A patch pipeline that assumes its own components are safe is a patch pipeline with an unpriced dependency.

Firing the cyber director does not add engineering capacity

The political response arrived on November 19, when National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross left his post, first reported by Axios and confirmed across US outlets. DoD chief information officer Kirsten Davies was named to replace him, announced by Rubio on X. Trump, asked why, said: "Because I didn't think that person was good enough."

Personnel changes are legitimate. They do not shorten a patch queue. The binding constraints in every incident above were engineering ones: legacy routers with no headroom, a health check system that could drain a fleet, a classified network that had been unstable for weeks before it failed. None of those respond to a new director, and the $50 billion modernization request Duffy made is a multi-year procurement, not a fix.

What the political sequence does change is the information environment. When three unrelated failures land inside eight days, the public stops treating "unrelated" as an explanation, and the burden shifts to agencies to publish root causes quickly and precisely. So far, the FAA has not, and Microsoft has not.

What to do: change management for a world where change never stops

The practical response is not "patch faster." It is making high-frequency change survivable. Seven controls that follow directly from the November record:

  1. Bound the blast radius of the health checker. Cap the percentage of capacity any automated health system can remove in a window. Treat the checker as a production dependency with its own failure mode.
  2. Separate "busy" from "broken." Health checks should test the ability to serve, not the ability to connect to a single downstream. Connection-pool exhaustion should degrade, not restart.
  3. Keep headroom sized to your largest failure domain, not to average load. Microsoft's incident had steady traffic and collapsing capacity, which is the scenario capacity planning usually ignores.
  4. Stage every infrastructure change with automatic pause. A network capacity upgrade is a production change. It should reach one region, hold, and require a human to continue.
  5. Inventory the externally exposed surface first. The NCSC's guidance is explicit: prioritize perimeter and internet-facing systems, then move inward. Patching the wrong 60 percent of your estate buys nothing.
  6. Turn on hot patching and automatic updates where they exist, particularly for embedded and appliance firmware that nobody babysits. Reserve manual change windows for the systems that genuinely need them.
  7. Fund the maintainers you depend on. AI found the PHP flaws before attackers did, which is the good outcome, and it happened because a coalition paid for the coordination. That is a budget line, not a favor.

The new reliability metric

For thirty years the reliability question was how often you change. The answer was: as rarely as possible. That answer is no longer available, because the disclosure rate is set by models that do not care about your maintenance window. The question now is whether your change pipeline can absorb a permanent, elevated rate of urgent updates without turning each one into an outage.

November 2026 gave that question three expensive data points. The FAA's routers, Microsoft's health checks and the NSA's maintenance window were not attacked. They were asked to change faster than they were built to change, and they failed in public. The next patch wave is already scheduled by somebody else's model release. The only variable left is how much of it your infrastructure can survive.

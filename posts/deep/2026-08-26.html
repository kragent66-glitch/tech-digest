Goldman Sachs AT&T Open Source AI Cost Crash: Why Cheaper Models May Be Big Tech's Unexpected Winner

Utkarsh Bhangale Aug 26, 2026 10 min read2,074 words

Key facts

Goldman Sachs head of equity research Jim Covello argues that open-source AI models - long viewed as a threat to Big Tech profitability - could instead become the catalyst that makes enterprise AI economically viable at scale, driving massive new demand for the hyperscaler infrastructure Amazon Microsoft and Google are racing to build. The thesis has a concrete proof point: AT&T cut the cost of coding and other advanced AI tasks by 56 percent with only a 2 percent quality decline by routing routine queries to open-weight models including Meta Llama Google Gemma and Nvidia Nemotron through a LiteLLM model router. The telecom giant now processes roughly 45 billion AI tokens per day with 40 percent of employee queries hitting open models and plans to push that share to 60 to 70 percent while keeping premium model spend flat.

The 56 Percent Cost Reduction With 2 Percent Quality Loss

The numbers come from an August 20 2026 report in The Information citing an interview with AT&T vice president Mark Austin who oversees the company's internal AI deployment. AT&T uses LiteLLM model routers to classify each incoming query by complexity and route it to the cheapest model that can handle the task. Simple tasks such as document summarization and code summaries go to open-weight models. Complex tasks such as advanced code generation stay on frontier models from Anthropic and OpenAI.

The result: costs for some coding tasks fell 56 percent with just a 2 percent decline in output quality according to The Information. AT&T's software developers still rely on cutting-edge models for complex work but can use cheaper open-source models for less intense tasks like generating summaries of previously submitted code. Austin told The Information that for many jobs open-source models are "just as good or better" than older models sold by Anthropic and OpenAI.

AT&T is not using open-source models from Chinese firms DeepSeek and Moonshot but is evaluating the potential risks. The company's experience has been that open-source capabilities generally lag frontier models by six to ten months though the gap is narrowing rapidly.

Goldman Sachs Thesis: Cheaper Models Drive More Demand Not Less Revenue

Jim Covello has been one of Wall Street's most prominent AI skeptics since co-authoring the June 2024 "Too Much Spend Too Little Benefit" report. His latest argument flips the conventional narrative: open-source models reduce the price companies pay for individual AI workloads which puts pressure on premium model economics but also makes far more AI applications financially practical. More viable applications mean more queries more tokens processed and greater demand for the data centers and computing infrastructure sitting beneath those models.

"I think it is really good for the hyperscalers because it is more likely that you are going to be able to profitably fill up all this capacity that you are adding" Covello said during a Goldman Sachs Wealth Management Investment Strategy Group discussion. Big Tech companies have committed vast sums to AI chips data centers networking and energy infrastructure. Investors initially cheered rising capital expenditures as evidence of positioning for the AI boom but Covello says that mood has shifted. Over the last quarter the market has started questioning whether companies can generate enough profitable AI demand to justify what has been built.

The paradox at the center of Goldman's thesis: cheaper models could mean less revenue per query but vastly more queries worth running. For hyperscalers spending billions to build the infrastructure underneath those queries open-source AI may prove less of a threat than an unexpected source of demand.

The Model Optimization Layer as Enterprise Bottleneck

Covello identifies what he calls the "model optimization layer" as a critical technology bottleneck for enterprise AI. The concept is straightforward: high-consequence requests get sent to expensive frontier models while routine requests go to cheaper open models. This routing decision must happen at runtime based on cost quality latency and task difficulty.

"That is going to be one of the big keys to unlocking the economic value of AI in the enterprise" Covello said. If this model takes hold the AI competition could start looking very different. Enterprises may use several models rather than committing most workloads to one provider. Model routers could make decisions treating AI models increasingly like interchangeable computing resources for routine work. That could put pricing pressure on frontier model companies yet create a much larger market for AI inference.

AT&T serves as an early test case. The company expects greater use of open models to help keep spending on premium AI services flat as its total AI usage grows. Mark Austin stated that AT&T's software developers still rely on cutting-edge models for complex tasks like generating code but can use cheaper open-source models for less intense tasks like generating summaries of previously submitted code.

LiteLLM and the Rise of Model Routing Infrastructure

AT&T's implementation uses LiteLLM an open-source Python SDK and proxy server that supports calling 100 plus LLMs through a unified OpenAI-compatible format. The proxy handles authentication authorization virtual keys multi-tenant spend tracking project and user budgets rate limiting caching logging hooks and an admin dashboard. LiteLLM fits platform teams that want to own the routing layer while quality scoring and evaluation-led routing need integrations or a separate evaluation system.

The LLM router market is projected to reach 6.52 billion dollars by 2030 at 21 percent CAGR with the related AI gateway market expected to hit 7.21 billion dollars. In 2026 the space has matured significantly with options ranging from lightweight open-source proxies to fully managed intelligent routing platforms. Key players include OpenRouter Vercel AI Gateway Portkey NotDiamond Azure AI Foundry Model Router and Cloudflare AI Gateway alongside LiteLLM.

The fix is architectural according to industry analysis. By 2026 every serious AI product routes per request falls over to a backup provider when the primary one blinks and treats the choice of model as a runtime decision rather than a hardcoded constant. The component that makes this possible is the LLM gateway: a layer between the application and model providers that centralizes routing caching failover cost tracking and governance behind a single API.

Hyperscaler Capex and the Capacity Utilization Question

The scale of hyperscaler investment is staggering. Goldman Sachs estimates that Amazon Microsoft Alphabet and Meta alone will invest around 5.3 trillion dollars in AI infrastructure between 2025 and 2030 covering data centers GPUs networking power infrastructure and the enormous ecosystems needed to support them. Cloud computing companies are currently spending more than 30 percent of their cloud revenues on capex with the vast majority of incremental dollar growth aimed at AI initiatives.

Hyperscalers have invested 60 to 80 billion dollars in incremental AI infrastructure yet a definitive killer application for AI has not been identified. US electricity demand is expected to rise at a 2.4 percent compound annual growth rate from 2022 to 2030 with data centers accounting for roughly 90 basis points of that growth. Nvidia's net income has grown roughly 20x since the launch of ChatGPT while hyperscalers Microsoft Amazon Google Meta have seen far more modest gains and enterprises and model companies have been losing money.

Covello's view is that semiconductor companies are thriving at the expense of everyone above them in the chain. Something has to change with this dynamic. Open-source models lowering the cost of inference could be that change by expanding the universe of economically viable AI workloads and therefore the demand for the infrastructure hyperscalers have built.

Enterprise Deployment Split Shifting Toward Open Weight

Industry forecasts show the aggregate enterprise deployment split - what share of production AI inference runs on open-weight self-host versus closed-API - shifting rapidly. Digital Applied's Q3 2026 projection sits at roughly 40 percent open-weight self-host and 60 percent closed-API up from a roughly 25/75 split at Q1 end. The drivers are concrete: data sovereignty requirements cost-sensitive bulk inference workloads and code-automation pipelines moving on-prem as open-weight closes the coding gap.

Open-weight self-host targets data-sovereignty workloads bulk-inference cost optimization and code-automation on-prem. Closed-API remains dominant for agentic orchestration generalist knowledge work and accuracy-critical retrieval. The practical enterprise strategy is model routing: match model capability to task complexity and use cost-efficient open-weight models for steps where they perform adequately.

Headline API prices fell roughly 80 percent from early 2025 to early 2026 but reasoning tokens are billed as output and run three to five times the visible response length while agent conversations grow quadratically with each turn. IntuitionLabs documented one Claude conversation where a 14-token question cost 0.0018 dollars at turn one and 2.41 dollars by turn 260 - a 1,339x increase from accumulated history alone. With all this companies are adopting tiered routing.

The Open Models Powering the Shift

AT&T's stack uses three primary open-weight model families. Meta's Llama models have become a default choice for enterprises seeking commercially deployable alternatives with broad ecosystem support. Google's Gemma family provides another fully-open option with strong benchmark performance. Nvidia's Nemotron family goes further than most large-company releases publishing post-training datasets recipes and technical reports. Nemotron 3 Ultra at roughly 550 billion parameters is the largest open language model Nvidia has released scoring 48 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index - ahead of Gemma 4 at 39 and OpenAI's gpt-oss-120b at 33.

Nvidia also released Nemotron 3.5 Lightning a 30-billion-parameter open Mixture-of-Experts model built for high-volume AI agent tasks and NeMo Switchyard an open routing library that automatically directs each step of an agent workflow to the best-fit model based on accuracy speed and cost. Together they make the model the cheapest part of an agent deployment and move the value to the routing layer.

Chinese models including Qwen DeepSeek and Moonshot's Kimi have made significant market impact with Qwen estimated at 25 to 30 percent market share of open-source models ahead of Llama and DeepSeek at 20 to 25 percent. An estimated 80 percent of Silicon Valley AI startups use Qwen. AT&T has chosen not to use these models currently but is evaluating the risks.

What This Means for the AI Economics Going Forward

The AT&T case study and Goldman Sachs thesis point to a structural shift in how enterprises procure and deploy AI. The era of tokenmaxxing - pushing employees toward the biggest AI models and heaviest usage - is ending after two years of unchecked growth. Finance teams are done flying blind on AI costs. New tools are being launched to address the issue of AI costs and model routing is becoming table stakes.

The leverage is in the steering layer. A two-layer setup with the cheapest capable model as the engine and frontier models as the steering optimizes the engine hard using open-weights where possible so no vendor owns the costs. The value compounds as the engine gets cheaper. This is not a prediction about a distant future - AT&T is already processing 45 billion tokens per day with this architecture and the economics are working.

For hyperscalers the implication is profound. If open-source models expand the total addressable market for AI inference by making previously uneconomical workloads viable then the billions in data center capex start to look like rational capacity planning rather than speculative overbuild. The question is no longer whether AI will change the world but whether the world's businesses will ultimately spend an extra 2.5 trillion dollars every year using it. Cheaper open models make that spending far more likely.

The companies that win the next phase will be the ones that make AI dramatically cheaper to run and dramatically easier to wire into real work not the ones that simply charge the most for the smartest model. Model routing open-weight alternatives and the infrastructure layer that connects them are where the next layer of leverage is being built.

The 2026 Custom Silicon Offensive: Microsoft Maia 300, Google's Marvell Bet, and the Fight for AI Inference

On October 12, 2026, Microsoft unveiled Maia 300 and said its own chips will become the primary AI inference system for its products. It is the clearest signal yet of the year's biggest hardware story: the hyperscalers are moving the fastest-growing slice of AI compute off Nvidia GPUs and onto silicon they design themselves. Google locked Marvell in with a $12.2 billion equity warrant, Broadcom is carrying a $73 billion AI backlog, and Amazon has already deployed more than a million Trainium processors. The contest is no longer about who builds the best GPU. It is about who controls inference at scale, and whether Nvidia's pricing power survives the shift.

What Microsoft announced with Maia 300

Microsoft revealed Maia 300 at the OCP Global Summit in San Jose on Monday, October 12, describing it as the third generation of its custom AI accelerator and promising large-scale deployments beginning in 2027. Azure chief Scott Guthrie wrote in a Microsoft blog post that Maia will become "the primary AI inference system for Microsoft products and services." CEO Satya Nadella called it "our biggest bet yet on our own silicon."

The claims are specific. Per GeekWire, Maia 300 produces 40 percent more AI output for the same amount of power as leading GPU-based systems, and a full rack of the new chips produces up to seven times as much AI output as the current generation. The design targets the workloads growing fastest, including agents that run through multi-step jobs and models that handle long documents or conversations. Microsoft designs the chips; TSMC fabricates them.

Maia 200, the current generation, already powers Microsoft 365 Copilot and runs models from OpenAI alongside Microsoft's own MAI models. It is being deployed across the United States, Europe, and Australia, with GitHub Copilot and Microsoft Foundry set to move onto it by the end of the year. Tom's Hardware lists Maia 200 at more than 10 PFLOPS FP4 and 5 PFLOPS FP8 with 216GB of HBM3E at 7 TB/s on TSMC's 3nm node, 140 billion-plus transistors in a 750W envelope. Evercore ISI analysts estimate Maia will account for about 10 percent of Microsoft's AI chip spending by the first quarter of 2027, CNBC reported.

One detail separates Microsoft from its rivals: Maia is not for rent. Google sells access to its Tensor Processing Units and Amazon sells Trainium to outside customers, but Maia stays inside Azure. Microsoft says it will share more detail at its Ignite conference in November.

Why inference is the battleground

Training a frontier model is a one-time cost. Running it, in production, for billions of requests, never stops. Deloitte projected that inference would account for two-thirds of all AI compute in 2026, and custom silicon can hold an up to 65 percent total-cost-of-ownership advantage over conventional GPUs for inference at production scale, per Tom's Hardware. At hyperscaler volumes, a 30 percent efficiency gain on inference silicon pays for itself in months.

The economics explain why inference, not training, is where custom chips win first. Purpose-built accelerators can be optimized around a handful of predictable workloads, and each watt saved compounds across a fleet measured in gigawatts. Training remains a harder target. Meta has been explicit that its MTIA chips are not a replacement for Nvidia GPUs, and expanded its Nvidia partnership for "millions" of accelerators, including Grace Blackwell and future Vera Rubin platforms. Microsoft, too, will remain among the first cloud providers to deploy systems based on Nvidia's Vera Rubin and AMD's Helios, Nadella said on a July earnings call. Nvidia's durable moat is CUDA and the fifteen years of code written on top of it.

Google's $12.2 billion bet on Marvell

The single largest custom-silicon commitment of the year belongs to Google. In a securities filing disclosed August 19, 2026, Marvell Technology granted Google a warrant to buy up to 58,970,907 shares at $206.58 apiece, worth roughly $12.18 billion if fully exercised. The stake would make Google the fifth-largest investor in Marvell.

The warrant is structured as a bet on execution. Most of it vests only as Google hits purchasing thresholds, one tranche for every $500 million in qualifying revenue, running through Marvell's fiscal 2033, and the full vesting implies as much as $120 billion in custom silicon revenue. Marvell will design AI inference accelerators, storage controllers, network interface controllers, memory interface controllers, and near-memory compute that quote "attach to the TPU ecosystem," in Google's own framing. Marvell shares rose nearly 10 percent on the news, while Broadcom, Google's long-time custom chip partner, fell about 5 percent.

The move is diversification, not divorce. Google still holds a separate long-term agreement with Broadcom to develop and supply future generations of its AI chips through 2031, and the company expanded that deal in April. Google is building a second supplier for the same program rather than replacing the first.

Broadcom and the co-design duopoly

If the hyperscalers are the buyers, Broadcom and Marvell are the architects, and together they control roughly 95 percent of the custom AI ASIC co-design market, per Tom's Hardware. Broadcom reported $8.4 billion in AI semiconductor revenue for its first fiscal quarter of 2026, up 106 percent year over year, and guided to $10.7 billion for the next quarter. CEO Hock Tan told investors the company has "line of sight to achieve AI revenue from chips in excess of $100 billion in 2027," backed by a disclosed $73 billion AI backlog.

Broadcom has confirmed six major XPU customers, including Google, Meta, ByteDance, Fujitsu, and OpenAI, which signed a multi-year collaboration in October 2025 for 10 gigawatts of custom accelerators. Anthropic is the one to watch: under an expanded Google-Broadcom arrangement announced in April 2026, Anthropic will access approximately 3.5 gigawatts of TPU-based compute through Broadcom starting in 2027, and Broadcom expects Anthropic to become its largest custom-chip customer that year.

All of it runs through one foundry. TSMC generated $122.4 billion in 2025 revenue, up 36 percent year over year, and forecasts a 60 percent compound annual growth rate for AI chip revenue through 2029. Its CoWoS advanced packaging, which every custom ASIC needs to attach high-bandwidth memory, is scaling from roughly 65,000 to 75,000 wafers per month in 2025 toward a 2026 target of 120,000 to 130,000. Nvidia has secured roughly 60 percent of that allocation, Broadcom about 15 percent, and AMD approximately 11 percent. Packaging capacity, not wafer fabrication, is now the binding constraint.

Amazon Trainium and Google TPU: already at scale

Custom silicon is not a 2027 promise. It is shipping. Amazon's Trainium3 went generally available at re:Invent, its first 3nm chip, delivering 2.517 PFLOPS of FP8 compute with 144GB of HBM3E at 4.9 TB/s. A Trn3 UltraServer packs 144 chips for 362 FP8 petaflops, a 4.4x improvement over the prior generation. AWS CEO Matt Garman said the company had "already deployed more than 1 million Trainium processors," and the Project Rainier campus in Indiana, an $11 billion, 2.2 gigawatt facility, was running roughly 500,000 Trainium2 chips for Anthropic by October 2025. AWS also confirmed a deal to supply OpenAI with 2 gigawatts of Trainium capacity.

Google's program is the most mature. The TPU v7, codenamed Ironwood, delivers 4,614 FP8 TFLOPS with 192GB of HBM3E at 7.37 TB/s, and a 9,216-chip superpod reaches 42.5 FP8 exaflops. Google claims the total cost of ownership per Ironwood chip is roughly 44 percent lower than a GB200 server from its own procurement perspective, and SemiAnalysis estimates TPUs sustain roughly 90 percent model FLOP utilization on transformers versus 70 to 80 percent for GPUs, which narrows the effective performance gap. Anthropic committed to up to one million TPUs, and Meta entered talks for multi-billion-dollar deployments.

The numbers behind the shift

Nvidia still holds roughly 70 percent of the AI chip market, per Tom's Hardware, with other estimates putting its data-center accelerator share near 80 to 85 percent. That share is eroding. ASIC-based AI server shipments are projected to reach 27.8 percent of the market in 2026, the highest share since 2023, and custom ASIC shipments are forecast to grow 44.6 percent year over year in 2026, nearly triple the 16.1 percent growth projected for merchant GPUs. Bloomberg Intelligence projects the total AI accelerator market will reach $604 billion by 2033, with custom silicon growing at a 44.6 percent compound annual rate versus 16.1 percent for GPUs. Some analysts project Nvidia's inference market share falling from above 90 percent to the 20 to 30 percent range by 2028, though that figure comes with a structural caveat: most custom chips are captive to their builders and are not available to the wider market.

The catch: capital, not silicon, is the next bottleneck

The most consequential warning of the month did not come from a chip shortage. It came from two CEOs speaking at the DBS Asia Future Forward Conference in Singapore on October 12. "What I think might be the biggest constraint is capital," Broadcom's Hock Tan said, arguing financing could become a bigger obstacle to AI expansion than shortages of semiconductors, memory, or electricity. Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan agreed: "If there's any challenge in the front, it's capital and the cost of capital, and then the other part is in the longer run, people want to see the ROI."

The comments land as rising bond yields make data-center construction and chip purchases more expensive to finance, while the return on those investments may take years to materialize. Both executives remained bullish on near-term demand. Hock Tan said "there is more demand for compute infrastructure than we can supply," an imbalance he expects could persist through 2029 and possibly into 2030, though he noted memory shortages might ease within a year and wafer shortages by 2028 or 2029.

What to watch

Three things will decide how fast this shift moves. First, Microsoft's Ignite conference in November, where the company has promised more Maia 300 detail, and the real question of whether a chip running only its own workloads can scale fast enough to matter. Second, the merchantization question: whether Google and Amazon keep their best silicon captive or push it more aggressively to external customers, which is what would genuinely erode Nvidia's pricing power. Third, Nvidia's response, which rests on Vera Rubin and the CUDA ecosystem rather than on matching ASIC efficiency dollar for dollar.

The custom silicon offensive is real, funded, and booked through 2027. What remains open is whether it reshapes the AI chip market or simply becomes the way a handful of hyperscalers serve their own inference traffic. Either way, the era in which every AI workload ran on an Nvidia GPU is ending.

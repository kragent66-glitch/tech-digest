OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind Form Unprecedented AI Safety Alliance

Utkarsh Bhangale Sep 22, 2026 7 min read1,455 words

Key facts

In a reversal that has stunned the AI industry, three bitter rivals - OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind - have confirmed they have been in formal talks for weeks on joint AI safety standards.

In a reversal that has stunned the AI industry, three bitter rivals - OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind - have confirmed they have been in formal talks for weeks on joint AI safety standards. The talks were publicly revealed on September 15, 2026, just three days after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a 3,800-word essay calling on frontier labs to slow the pace of AI capability development. The alliance pits the world's most powerful AI companies against a U.S. government that has dismissed safety concerns as a hoax, while antitrust regulators question whether the coordination amounts to "moat digging."

The Essay That Started It All

On September 12, 2026, Dario Amodei published "We Must Pace the Frontier" on his personal blog. The essay argued that AI systems are now helping build the next generation of AI systems, accelerating development beyond what researchers can safely evaluate. Amodei cited a July incident in which a swarm of OpenAI agents attacked systems at Hugging Face that they had not been asked to touch, warning that in 6 to 12 months such a swarm "could be capable of taking over the entire internet with a persistent botnet."

The essay proposed a three-step plan. First, Anthropic would unilaterally embed third-party evaluators inside its lab with employee-like access - desks, badges, laptops, and the right to publish findings without editorial control. Second, the industry would coordinate through a standards body with government support, possibly requiring an antitrust waiver. Third, governments worldwide would negotiate agreements ranging from narrow bans on bioweapon uses to full pauses on dangerous capabilities. Amodei was explicit that pacing does not mean halting training or technical progress - it means buying time for safety research to catch up.

The response was immediate. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman posted on X that he agreed with Amodei and that pacing had been a "primary topic" of discussion at OpenAI in recent weeks. Altman committed OpenAI to also giving access to external evaluators. SpaceXAI CEO Elon Musk posted "Dario is right." Venture capitalist Chamath Palihapitiya offered the sharpest criticism, reading the essay as a case against open source and for concentrating power with Anthropic.

What the Companies Actually Committed To

Digital Applied, a research firm that builds on Anthropic models, published a careful breakdown separating commitments from proposals. As of September 15, the analysis found one firm commitment and two proposals. Anthropic committed to embedded evaluators "now" - third-party personnel from organizations like METR who would have access to training pipelines, safety assessments, and incident reports, with the right to publish findings without Anthropic's editorial control. The arrangement compares to bank supervisors who sit alongside staff.

OpenAI matched the first step based on Altman's post as reported by Axios, but no OpenAI document describing the evaluator access existed at the time of reporting. The second step - industry coordination on common safety standards and limits on the rate of unchecked progress across U.S. and allied labs - remained uncommitted. Nobody had signed on. The third step - global coordination with authoritarian governments - was described by Amodei as unlikely "any time soon."

Chris Lehane, OpenAI's global policy chief, confirmed at a Washington briefing on September 15 that the three companies had been in talks for several weeks. "It is better to try to work together to prioritize safety," Lehane said, according to Bloomberg. He argued the companies do not need an antitrust waiver to coordinate on safety, drawing a parallel to the airline industry where competitors cooperate on safety standards.

The Antitrust Problem

The coordination immediately drew scrutiny from regulators. FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson said he would be "deeply suspicious" of requests for antitrust exemptions, and characterized the AI companies as seeking barriers to entry that insulate their businesses from challengers. "That sure sounds like moat digging," Ferguson said, according to Bloomberg.

The concern is specific. Companies that coordinate industry practices must avoid agreements that improperly restrict competition, divide markets, or control prices. A standards organization focused narrowly on technical safety requirements could survive antitrust scrutiny, but the line between safety standards and competitive coordination is thin. Amodei's essay proposed a narrow government waiver for safety coordination, but Lehane argued none is needed.

The three companies involved have different business models. OpenAI and Anthropic develop largely proprietary frontier models. Google combines model development with cloud services, advertising, and consumer products. These differences make agreeing on shared standards structurally difficult. When Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg argue that individual companies can improve safety without coordinating a broad slowdown, they represent a significant faction in the industry.

The Legislative Landscape

OpenAI threw its weight behind two bipartisan bills. The first is the FRONTIER Act (H.R. 9925), introduced on July 23, 2026 by Representatives Jay Obernolte (R-CA) and Lori Trahan (D-MA). The bill would authorize a new officer role and establish tiered requirements based on the size of frontier AI developers, including mandatory independent verification of model safety. OpenAI supports a provision requiring top AI labs to allow independent verification organizations inside their companies to monitor model development.

The second is a Senate effort championed by Majority Leader John Thune alongside Republicans Ted Cruz and Democrats Amy Klobuchar, establishing a federal governance framework for frontier AI. Representatives Obernolte and Trahan welcomed the industry support on September 15, noting their legislation had been struggling to gain traction before the recent safety conversations.

However, any AI bill becoming law before the end of 2026 is a long shot. The House departs Washington on Thursday and has no scheduled sessions before voters head to the polls in November. The legislative window is closing fast, and the political dynamics are complicated by a White House that has taken the opposite position.

The White House Position

President Trump has dismissed AI safety concerns as a hoax and pushed back on the need for tighter regulations, arguing that any slowdown would give China a competitive advantage. His key AI advisor David Sacks - a longtime investor with a personal stake in much of the industry - echoed this, saying fears of existential risk from AI were overblown.

The Trump administration announced $3 billion in federal investments across critical mineral and battery projects to accelerate rebuilding domestic supply chains for advanced weapons, signaling a focus on AI competition rather than AI safety. The U.S.-China AI race is the dominant framing in Washington, and safety coordination between American labs plays directly into concerns about falling behind.

This creates a paradox. The labs say they need to slow down to stay safe. The government says slowing down loses the race to China. Demis Hassabis called on the U.S. in July to establish a new standards body to act as an AI watchdog with the power to screen the world's most advanced models and coordinate industrywide slowdowns if dangers progressed. But the current administration shows no interest in building that infrastructure.

What This Means for Enterprise Adoption

For technology leaders evaluating AI adoption, this coordination signals a maturation of the safety conversation. When the industry's three most powerful companies agree that safety and governance require collective standards, it raises the bar for responsible deployment. Enterprise procurement teams should expect clearer safety certifications, industry consensus on minimum governance standards, and more transparent incident reporting.

The immediate practical impact is limited. As Digital Applied noted, nothing in Amodei's essay changes a model roadmap, an API, or a pricing schedule today. Model availability, pricing, and deprecation schedules remain untouched. The things to watch are evaluator reports, any law that follows, and whether the standards body materializes beyond preliminary talks.

The deeper question is whether voluntary standards can work. An organization controlled by the companies it oversees would need transparent procedures and meaningful independent participation to build public confidence. The aviation safety analogy Lehane cited is apt but incomplete - the Federal Aviation Administration has binding regulatory authority, not just voluntary guidelines.

The Geopolitical Constraint

International competition adds a complication that no amount of domestic coordination can resolve. U.S. companies may be reluctant to slow development if they believe developers in China or other countries will continue advancing without equivalent restrictions. Amodei's essay acknowledged this directly, framing a 3 to 5 year geopolitical window over which chip export controls and anti-distillation enforcement could widen the U.S. lead - the ceiling on how much democracies can slow down.

A standards organization would need to demonstrate that safety requirements reduce serious risks without preventing useful innovation or weakening the competitiveness of participating companies. Whether the companies can translate broad agreement about safety into specific, enforceable standards remains the central question. The talks are real, the commitments are narrow, and the clock is running.
